## What is PPBS?

The Psychology of Political Behavior Studies (PPBS) is a cumulative research program designed to explain how psychological dispositions and social identities shape political attitudes, participation, and voting. Across three U.S. election cycles—2016, 2018, and 2020—PPBS fielded 12 datasets through professional survey providers, combining large national quota samples, independent confirmatory samples, and longitudinal recontacts.

What makes PPBS distinctive is its breadth and its bridge between disciplines. The surveys pair measures commonly used in political science—party identification, ideology, political knowledge, participation, and vote choice—with full psychological instruments assessing constructs such as system justification, social dominance orientation, collective narcissism, epistemic and existential needs, and justice concerns. More than 60 political and psychological constructs can therefore be studied together rather than in isolation.

The design supports three complementary goals: discovering patterns in large, demographically diverse samples; testing whether those patterns recur in independent samples; and examining change within the same respondents over time. This combination makes PPBS useful for theory comparison, effect-size benchmarking, replication, and cumulative research.

## Why PPBS?

Political science and psychology often study the same political phenomena with different tools. Political science contributes strong traditions in election surveys, population-focused sampling, and behavioral measurement; psychology contributes validated multi-item scales, psychometrics, and theories of motivation and identity. PPBS brings these strengths into one design, continuing a long-standing call for more integrative political psychology ([Haste, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9221.2011.00868.x); [Huddy, Sears, & Levy, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780199760107.013.0001); [Rosenberg, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1468-5914.2003.00225.x)).

Its exploratory and confirmatory structure also responds to credibility concerns across the social sciences. Survey instruments are fielded in separate samples drawn from the same source, allowing promising associations to be tested again before they are treated as robust. Longitudinal recontacts add a further test: whether relationships observed between people are also visible as people change over time.

### 1. Build and compare theory

Political behavior can rarely be explained by a single construct. PPBS places competing explanations in the same dataset so researchers can compare their reach, overlap, and limits. This addresses what [Borsboom (2013)](https://osc.centerforopenscience.org/2013/11/20/theoretical-amnesia/) called “theoretical amnesia” and supports more deliberate theory construction ([Borsboom et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691620969647); [Muthukrishna & Henrich, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-018-0522-1); [Oberauer & Lewandowsky, 2019](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-019-01645-2)). It also offers a practical response to psychology's “toothbrush problem”: the tendency to prefer one's own theory to anyone else's ([Mischel, 2008](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/the-toothbrush-problem)).

### 2. Study large, diverse samples

Large national quota samples improve precision and make heterogeneity across demographic and political groups visible. They also help address the persistent problem of underpowered studies in psychology ([Maxwell, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1037/1082-989X.9.2.147); [Crutzen & Peters, 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01184)) while avoiding claims that quota sampling alone guarantees perfect population representation.

### 3. Use stronger measurement

Single items and convenient proxies can introduce avoidable measurement error and give researchers too many degrees of freedom. PPBS therefore prioritizes validated multi-item instruments and documents how constructs are operationalized, responding directly to calls to treat measurement as a central part of theory testing ([Fried & Flake, 2018](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/measurement-matters)).

### 4. Map nomological networks

Psychological constructs gain meaning through their relationships with other constructs. By measuring identities, motives, values, attitudes, and behavior together, PPBS lets researchers map those networks, test discriminant validity, and examine whether rival theories describe distinct processes or different views of the same process ([Rosenberg, 2003](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1468-5914.2003.00225.x); [Smaldino, 2017](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315173726-14)).

### 5. Test reproducibility

Independent confirmatory samples help distinguish stable findings from sample-specific results. That principle follows the credibility reforms in psychology and political science ([Vazire, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691617751884); [Wuttke, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11615-018-0131-7)) and the broader evidence on replication across the social sciences ([Camerer et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaf0918); [Klein, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0959354314529616)).

### 6. Benchmark effects

Realistic effect-size estimates are essential for power analyses, preregistrations, and registered reports. PPBS provides large-sample benchmarks while keeping the size of typical psychological effects—and the biases that can inflate them—in view ([Schäfer & Schwarz, 2019](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00813)).

### 7. Support cumulative science

Shared measures, transparent documentation, repeated sampling, and longitudinal recontacts make findings easier to compare, replicate, and extend. The goal is not one definitive dataset, but durable research infrastructure that helps political scientists and psychologists build knowledge together ([King, 1995](https://doi.org/10.2307/420301); [Smaldino, 2017](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315173726-14)).
